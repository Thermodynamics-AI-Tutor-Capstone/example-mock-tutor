## Problem

2. (20 points) The goal of this problem is to analyze James Prescott Joule's ground-breaking experiment where he really showed the first law and wrote about it in his seminal paper, "On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat." This problem will provide some historical context for how the first law was experimentally confirmed, and will show you calculations based on a real-life experiment that demonstrates the transfer of energy in a quantifiable way.
a. First, do some background research on the experiment itself and sketch it on your homework. Useful resources: http://www.aps.org/publications/apsnews/200912/physicshistory.cfm; http://twt.mpei.ac.ru/TTHB/2/KiSyShe/eng/Chapter2/2-1-Heat_Joule's-experiment-Equivalence-of-heat-and-work.html; https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MBrTDKc9YZ0; https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/epdf/10.1098/rstl.1850.0004
b. In Joule's paper, he says that the weights he used were 58 lbs. total. The weights were lifted to 45 inches 20 times. Calculate the total change in potential energy of the weights through the experiment in Joules.
c. Assume water is compressible and has a specific heat of $c=4.187$ kJ/kg-K. Using the approximation that $\Delta U=Mc\Delta T$ to estimate how much the temperature changed as a result of the weights falling 20 times. Assume the mass of the water is 7.84 lbs., as stated on page 70 of Joule's paper.
d. Compare that temperature difference to the measurements shown in the video (or to Joule's own measurements in the paper). Why might differences arise?
e. Using the initial temperature of the water from the video and the approximation $\Delta U=Mc\Delta T$, determine how far these weights would have to fall to boil the water.

## What it tests

- Students can convert US customary units to SI units
- Students can compute gravitational potential energy change
- Students can relate a measured work input to an internal energy rise
- Students can compare an ideal calculation with experimental measurements and explain discrepancies
- Students can compute a required falling distance for a specified temperature rise

## Assumptions the solution expects

- S.C.S.
- Quasi-equilibrium

## Where students go wrong

- Failing to convert pounds to kilograms or inches to meters before computing $Mg\Delta z$
- Forgetting that the weights are lifted 20 times, using 45 in instead of $20\times45$ in of total falling distance
- Using $c=4.187$ kJ/kg-K with $\Delta PE$ in J without converting units consistently
- Using the water mass in pounds instead of kilograms in $\Delta U=Mc\Delta T$
- Treating the measured video temperature rise as if it should exactly equal the ideal calculation, rather than explaining losses such as heat loss to the surrounding room

## How it is graded

Points reward the stated assumptions, correct unit conversions, $\Delta PE = Mg\Delta z$, energy equivalence $\Delta PE = M_{H_2O}c\Delta T$, reading the video temperatures, and a reasonable explanation for the difference.
