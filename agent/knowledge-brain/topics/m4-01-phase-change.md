# M4.1 — Phase Change

## What this lecture covers
This short lecture introduces phase change: why substances stop behaving as ideal gases when intermolecular forces matter, and how to read T-v and P-v diagrams, including the vapor dome, saturated states, critical point, and quality x (p. 1, p. 2, p. 5).

## Key ideas
- Intermolecular forces are negligible only in the ideal-gas limit, annotated as \(T_{mol} \ll T_{b/w\ mol}\) (p. 2).
- A piston-cylinder picture with external pressure \(P_{ext}\) and heat transfer \(Q\) sets up the physical situation for cooling/compressing a vapor toward condensation (p. 2).
- The T-v path labeled 1-2-3-4-5 traces ideal gas, non-ideal gas, formation of liquid, condensation to all liquid, and then cooled liquid (p. 2).
- Inside the vapor dome, the substance is a mixture of vapor and liquid; the annotated slide states that \(P\) and \(T\) are not independent there (p. 3).
- Different isobars show how the vapor dome changes with pressure; the lower isobar \(P_2\) shows saturated liquid at \(x=0\), saturated vapor at \(x=1\), and mixture states with \(0<x<1\) between them (p. 3).
- Quality is the mass fraction of vapor in a liquid-vapor mixture: \(x = M_g/(M_g+M_f)\) (p. 3, p. 5).
- The T-v diagram labels subcooled liquid, saturated liquid, mixture, saturated vapor, superheated vapor, and critical point (p. 5).
- The P-v diagram labels liquid, saturated liquid, mixture, saturated vapor, superheated vapor, critical point, and supercritical region (p. 5).

## Notation used
- \(T\): temperature; \(v\): specific volume; \(P\): pressure; \(T_{sat}\): saturation temperature line (p. 2, p. 5).
- \(M_g\): mass of vapor in the mixture; \(M_f\): mass of liquid in the mixture; \(x\): quality (p. 3, p. 5).

## Examples in this lecture
- The T-v state path 1-5 illustrates ideal-gas cooling, departure from ideal gas, condensation into a liquid-vapor mixture, complete condensation, and cooling of liquid (p. 2).
- The two-isobar T-v diagram illustrates how pressure changes the saturation dome and where \(x=0\), \(x=1\), and two-phase mixtures lie (p. 3).
