# M2.1 — Properties States

> Drafted by AI (DeepSeek, from transcripts of the course files; checked by Claude) — not reviewed by an instructor. Handwriting in the source was occasionally misread; the cited pages are the authority.

## What this lecture covers

This lecture defines what a property is, separates thermodynamic properties from ordinary properties, defines thermodynamic state, and applies those ideas to ice melting in a closed Tupperware container.

## Key ideas

- A property is a quantifiable, macroscopic characteristic of a system (p. 2). The annotations clarify: "quantifiable" means you can put a number to it; "macroscopic" means it applies to the bulk.
- Examples of properties include mass, volume, temperature, pressure, humidity, color, internal energy, entropy, and heat capacity (p. 4). The subset called thermodynamic properties "have to do with the energy of the system" (p. 4).
- The thermodynamic state of a system is defined by all of the system properties (p. 5).
- The ice-in-Tupperware example labels two snapshots State 1 and State 2 before and after melting (p. 6).
- Between State 1 and State 2, the recorded changes include temperature increasing, volume represented as $\forall$, and phase changing from solid to liquid; the mass remains equal, $M_1=M_2$ (p. 8).

## Notation used

- $\forall$ is used on the slide for total volume (p. 8). It is the script-V symbol, not a velocity or "for all" symbol in this context.
- Subscripts 1 and 2 label two states of the same system (pp. 6, 8).

## Examples in this lecture

- Ice in a closed Tupperware container: State 1 is ice and State 2 is liquid. The example asks which properties changed during melting and uses it to illustrate property and state language (pp. 6–8).
