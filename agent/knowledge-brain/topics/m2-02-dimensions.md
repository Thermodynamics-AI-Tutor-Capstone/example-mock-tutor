# M2.2 — Dimensions

> Drafted by AI (DeepSeek, from transcripts of the course files; checked by Claude) — not reviewed by an instructor. Handwriting in the source was occasionally misread; the cited pages are the authority.

## What this lecture covers
This deck introduces primary dimensions and their SI units, then derived dimensions and a unit breakdown for pressure, and finally SI prefixes with a bar-to-MPa conversion check.

## Key ideas
- Primary dimensions listed are mass ($M$, kg), length ($L$, m), time ($t$, s), temperature ($T$, K), amount of matter ($N$, moles), current ($I$, Amp), and luminosity ($C$, candela); the first five are marked as emphasized (p. 2).
- Derived dimensions are pressure $P$ in Pa, total volume $\mathcal{V}$ in m$^3$, velocity $v$ in m/s, and density $\rho$ in kg/m$^3$ (p. 3).
- Pressure is written as force per area, then expanded using $F=Ma$ (p. 3).
- The pascal is rewritten as N/m$^2$ and then in base units as kg/(m·s$^2$) (p. 3).
- SI prefixes listed are kilo $10^3$, mega $10^6$, centi $10^{-2}$, milli $10^{-3}$, and micro $10^{-6}$ (p. 4).
- Pressure conversion: $1\ \text{bar} = 100{,}000\ \text{Pa} = 100\ \text{kPa} = 0.1\ \text{MPa}$ (p. 4).
- The quick-check poll asks how many MPa correspond to 1 bar, with choices 100, 1, and 0.1 (p. 5).

## Notation used
- $M$, $L$, $t$, $T$, $N$, $I$, $C$: primary dimensions for mass, length, time, temperature, amount of matter, current, and luminosity (p. 2).
- $P$, $\mathcal{V}$, $v$, $\rho$: pressure, total volume, velocity, and density (p. 3). The slide uses a script V for total volume; this course writes total volume as $\mathcal{V}$.
- $F$, $A$, $a$, N, Pa, kg, m, s: force, area, acceleration, newton, pascal, kilogram, meter, second (p. 3). Note that $N$ is also used for amount of matter on p. 2; context distinguishes the two uses.
- Prefixes kilo, mega, centi, milli, micro and the pressure units bar, kPa, MPa (p. 4).

## Examples in this lecture
- Poll: "If 1 bar pressure is equal to 100,000 Pa, how many MPa is that?" with choices 100, 1, and 0.1 (p. 5). This demonstrates applying the bar/kPa/MPa conversion from p. 4.
