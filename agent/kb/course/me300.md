## What the course is

Engineering Thermodynamics I: basic thermodynamics concepts, properties of pure substances, and
first- and second-law analysis of systems and control volumes. Prerequisites are CHEM 110 and
MATH 141.

## Textbook — this matters more than it looks

**Turns & Pauley, *Thermodynamics — Concepts and Applications*, 2nd edition, Cambridge
University Press, 2020.** Key `turns-pauley-2e`.

Most thermodynamics material on the open internet — and most of what a language model has read —
assumes **Cengel & Boles**, whose chapter numbering is completely different. A bare "Chapter 3"
is therefore ambiguous and is forbidden in this knowledge base: every reading reference is
written as `{textbook: turns-pauley-2e, section: "2.7a"}`. If Kelvin ever finds itself about to
say "see Chapter 7 of your textbook", it should instead name the section from the card it is
reading, or say it does not know.

## The mandated solution format

The syllabus states this twice and applies it to **all homework and exam problems**. Kelvin
should use these six headings for any full worked solution, and should coach students into them
rather than around them.

1. **KNOWN** — list properties, variables, etc. given in the problem.
2. **FIND** — what is unknown that we are asked to find.
3. **SKETCH** — schematic diagram and relevant graphs (P-v, T-s diagrams, etc.).
4. **ASSUMPTIONS** — these need to be justified if they are atypical.
5. **ANALYSIS** — solve step-by-step, always include units, box the final answer.
6. **SANITY CHECK** — miscalculations can lead to unrealistic results; say so if the answer
   looks unrealistic.

The syllabus adds: *"the most important thing is that the solution method is coherent and
systematic. One of the major tools you should leave this course with is enhanced engineering
problem solving methodology."* That is the pedagogical goal the whole format serves — a student
who gets the right number by an incoherent route has not met it.
